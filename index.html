Affordable Housing: A Constitutional Conundrum

Hanover can meet its housing obligations and still decide its own future, if the Township, not developers, sets the vision.

By Ahmad Saleh, RN, CCRNDemocratic candidate for Hanover Township Committee

Share this article

Affordable housing debates in New Jersey date back to the 1920s. Before then, courts repeatedly struck down local zoning ordinances because nothing in the state constitution authorized them. In 1927, a Republican-majority legislature proposed, and the voters ratified, an amendment to the 1844 Constitution allowing the legislature to grant zoning power to municipalities. The 1947 Constitution, drafted under Republican Governor Alfred Driscoll and ratified by the voters, carried that same principle forward: zoning is an exercise of the state’s police power, and municipalities zone only under the laws the legislature enacts.

So even prior to the Mount Laurel doctrine, zoning was clearly established as a state police power. Delegation to municipalities does not absolve the power of the state; instead, it allows the municipality to act in its stead, and the state may intervene when it feels it is necessary. This is a constitutional power of the State, and specifically the legislature.

In 1975, the New Jersey Supreme Court’s Mount Laurel decision held that the state constitution requires developing municipalities to provide a realistic opportunity for their fair share of housing for low- and moderate-income families. The legislature followed with the Fair Housing Act of 1985, and over the years, most recently with the 2024 reforms, these requirements have become more and more stringent and prescriptive. Although we accept that constitutionally zoning is a state police power, the State is not involved in the day-to-day operations of municipal governance, and therefore certain requirements can be seen as too restrictive and at times arbitrary.

My position on discriminatory practice in housing development and zoning is clear: it is unacceptable and the state should not accept it. Housing is a basic human right, and no family should be zoned out of a community because of their income, their race, or where they come from. For the most part, this is a New Jerseyan belief and one that is bipartisan. The disagreement is over how it’s implemented. Some will argue the legislature has gone too far, and in certain areas it may have. But how the state interprets its constitutional authority is outside the scope of my potential authority as a committee member, and it is not the topic of discussion.

The discussion here is whether Hanover Township is meeting its obligations in a manner that benefits Hanover as best as possible, creates opportunity for our neighbors, ensures there is no discrimination in zoning, and establishes a deliberate vision for Hanover where developers are required to compete for projects and development rather than developers driving the vision.

Who is driving the vision?

Currently, we have a process where developers drive the vision and the municipality has not been meaningfully acting on behalf of the Township. Developers will build a 400-unit complex with 80 units as affordable housing units, yet the impact of the additional 320 units on traffic, noise, congestion, our schools, and municipal services is not taken into account.

Additionally, when abatements are granted, the contribution of those 320 units to tax revenue is minimal, and payments in lieu of taxes do not go to our schools. Too often, the township approves variances and abatements for the same developers without much competition or transparency. This is what has residents up in arms about the current strategy adopted by the Township Committee.

While the solution is complex and multifactorial, there are several tactics that can help us develop a proper housing model that not only meets our obligations but helps elevate Hanover. This is part of my Hanover 2036 vision, a 10-year plan to develop Hanover into a self-sustaining economy allowing every resident to prosper:

  1. Revisiting our master plan in a manner that develops Hanover. A town center has been part of our master plan for at least 20 years, yet it still doesn’t exist, and changes to the plan have been piecemeal in approach rather than part of a deliberate vision. We can finally create a downtown with residences above retail and commercial space, including affordable housing units and fully affordable buildings developed through public-private partnerships and state and federal housing grants. Not only will this help us meet our housing requirements without the need for hundreds of additional market-rate units, but developers will also be building retail and commercial space, which will benefit the developers, create a true downtown for Hanover where families can congregate, increase business and commercial revenue for Hanover, and help us meet and potentially exceed our housing requirements. Affordable homes should be part of the heart of our town, not set apart from it.

  2. Hanover is not affordable for younger couples and starter families, and it is out of reach for young people who were raised in town but now have to live elsewhere. We can change that by building moderately priced townhomes and single-family homes for first-time buyers under certain income thresholds, and pairing them with first-time homebuyer programs and outreach so the families who grew up here know these opportunities exist. Under state fair housing rules, affordable units must be open to everyone in our region, and they should be. The goal is not to keep anyone out; it’s to make sure the people who built their lives here are not priced out.

  3. We need to work with the NJ League of Municipalities and the State Legislature aggressively to bring the concerns and impacts of one-size-fits-all housing quotas to the state. Currently, most of these conversations are being led by Republicans, and this has become a partisan issue when in fact it’s not partisan. This is a New Jersey issue. We need to work with the legislature, create alliances, and tell the story, not from a “we don’t want more people” standpoint, but from the freedom to build our townships in a manner that suits the township while still meeting the needs of our neighbors who are lower income.

  4. Finally, we need to work with the legislature to reform the builder’s remedy, and if it cannot be reformed, replace it with a non-partisan Fair Housing & Development Commission with real authority to audit projects, hold both developers and municipalities accountable, and resolve disputes before they reach a courtroom. Developers are not building projects because they are concerned with affordable housing. They are building projects to serve their pockets, and rather than the remedy being used for its intended purpose, it is too often used to strong-arm townships into approving projects that are suboptimal for our communities, requiring townships to spend hundreds of thousands of tax dollars on legal fees rather than reinvesting in our communities.

A home comes first

Affordable housing is certainly a very important subject. As a nurse, I’ve learned and wholeheartedly practice Maslow’s Hierarchy of Needs: housing, safety, and security are fundamental requirements for human development and survival. A family cannot thrive, a child cannot learn, and a senior cannot age with dignity without a safe and stable home. We need to balance this to meet the needs of our residents who are less fortunate, while also ensuring townships have adequate autonomy to exercise their delegated power in the most efficient and cost-effective manner for our towns.
